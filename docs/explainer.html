Technical explainer · shapefit 0.1.0

How shapefit works

shapefit takes a shape with bad edges and gives back a clean polygon. A neural network does the difficult part. This page explains each step, from the training data to the final corners.

01The problem

Many shapes must be polygons, but the data shows them with bad edges.

Examples are building outlines from aerial photos, rooms in scanned floor plans, and parts in engineering drawings. A computer often finds these shapes as a mask. A mask is an image where each pixel is 1 (inside the shape) or 0 (outside the shape).

A mask from real data has problems on its edges:

  • Bumps and dents. The edge goes out or in where it must be straight.
  • Jagged edges. The edge has many small steps and spikes.
  • Gaps and bites. Parts of the shape are missing.
  • Rounded corners. Sharp corners become curves.
  • Noise. There are holes inside and loose dots outside.

We want a polygon: a closed shape made of straight edges that meet at corners. We want the correct number of corners, at the correct positions.

Why the usual tool fails

The usual tool is Douglas–Peucker simplification. It starts with the full outline of the mask. Then it removes points that are nearer than a distance ε (epsilon) to a straight line through their neighbours.

Douglas–Peucker can only remove points. It cannot move a point, and it does not know which points are real corners. Thus it must make a bad choice:

  • A small ε keeps the bumps. You get too many corners.
  • A large ε removes the bumps, but it also cuts off real corners.

02The idea: learn to remove the damage

It is easy to make damaged shapes when you know the clean shape first. A network can learn from these pairs.

Real data does not tell us the correct polygon. Thus we make our own data. This is synthetic data.

  1. Make a random clean polygon. We know its corners exactly.
  2. Draw it as a mask.
  3. Damage the mask with random bumps, jagged edges, gaps and noise.
  4. Give the damaged mask to the network as the input.
  5. Give the clean polygon to the network as the target: the correct answer.

The network sees millions of these pairs. It learns how the damage looks. Then it learns to give back the clean shape.

NOTE

Each training pair is new. The generator makes one pair in approximately 1 millisecond. Thus the network never sees the same example two times during a full training run.

03Clean polygons

The generator makes six types of polygon. Each polygon must have clear corners.

TypeShareDescription
ngon15 %Regular polygon with 3 to 8 corners. It can be a little stretched.
rectangle15 %Rotated rectangle. The long side is 1 to 4 times the short side.
quad12 %Trapezoid, parallelogram or kite.
convex18 %The convex hull of random points. 5 to 11 corners.
star18 %A star-shaped polygon with 5 to 12 corners. Some corners point in.
rectilinear22 %Two to four rectangles joined together: L, T, U and + shapes. These look like buildings.

A corner is only useful if a person can see it. Thus the generator rejects a polygon if it breaks one of these rules:

  • Each corner must turn by 12° or more. A smaller turn angle (the change in direction at a corner) looks like a straight line.
  • Each edge must be 6 % of the shape size or longer.
  • No corner can be sharper than a 15° spike.
  • The area must be 8 % of the bounding box or more. This rejects thin slivers.
  • The edges must not cross each other.

04The damage

Fifteen types of damage copy the problems in real masks. Each type can be used alone or with the others.

The damage occurs in two stages. Each damage type has a name and a probability. A random value called severity controls how strong the damage is. Severity is near 1 on average. Some shapes get almost no damage. Some get very bad damage.

Stage 1: Geometric damage

The generator puts 600 points along the outline at equal distances. Then it moves each point in or out, along the normal of the edge.

NameProbabilityEffect
round_corners35 %Makes corners into curves.
jagged75 %Moves each point by a small random distance.
bumps65 %Adds 1 to 5 smooth bumps (out) or dents (in).
wobble40 %Adds slow waves along the edge.
staircase20 %Moves groups of points by the same distance. This makes steps.

Stage 2: Pixel damage

The generator draws the outline as a mask. Then it changes pixels directly.

NameProbabilityEffect
bites50 %Removes 1 to 4 round parts from the edge.
gaps30 %Removes a strip along 4 to 15 % of the edge.
blobs40 %Adds 1 to 3 ovals on the edge.
cuts15 %Draws a thin empty line through the shape.
holes25 %Removes 1 to 4 round parts inside the shape.
speckle30 %Changes random pixels near the edge.
specks15 %Adds small dots away from the shape.
pixelate25 %Makes the image coarse, so edges become large steps.
blur30 %Blurs the mask, then makes it black and white again.
morph20 %Opens or closes small gaps with morphology.
NOTE

To apply only some damage types, use the only argument. Example: raster_corrupt(mask, rng, 92, only=["bites", "gaps"]).

05Framing: one standard view

The network always sees the shape at the same size and in the same position.

The input can have any size. A building can be 40 pixels wide or 4000 pixels wide. A network works best when all inputs look similar. Thus shapefit first moves and scales every shape onto a canvas. The canvas is a fixed image of 128 × 128 pixels.

  1. Find the bounding box of the shape. Ignore very small dots (less than 2 % of the largest part).
  2. Scale the shape equally in x and y. The longest side of the box becomes 72 % of the canvas (approximately 92 pixels).
  3. Put the centre of the box at the centre of the canvas.
  4. Keep the scale and the shift. Use them at the end to move the polygon back to the input coordinates.

The training data uses the same framing. In training, the box comes from the damaged shape, because only the damaged shape is available when you use the tool. Training also adds small random changes: ±8 % in scale and ±2 pixels in position. Thus the network does not need perfect framing.

CAUTION

A different framing in training and in use makes the network much less accurate. If you change SIZE or FILL, change them for training and for use together.

06The network

A U-Net looks at the canvas and makes two images: a clean mask and a corner map.

A neural network is a large mathematical function with many adjustable numbers, called weights. Training changes the weights until the outputs are near the targets. This network has 7.8 million weights.

shapefit uses a U-Net. A U-Net has two halves:

  • The encoder makes the image smaller in four steps (128 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 pixels). At each step, it keeps more channels. Thus it sees larger areas and understands the full shape.
  • The decoder makes the image larger again, back to 128 pixels.
  • Skip connections send fine detail from each encoder step directly to the decoder step of the same size. Thus the output keeps sharp positions.

Two extra input channels

The input has three channels: the mask, the x position of each pixel, and the y position of each pixel. This method is CoordConv. Without it, the network cannot easily know where a pixel is on the canvas.

Two outputs

  1. The clean mask tells where the true edge is, pixel by pixel.
  2. The corner heatmap tells where the corners are. Each pixel has a value from 0 to 1. A high value means "a corner is here".

The network gives each output as a logit. shapefit changes logits to probabilities from 0 to 1 when it needs them.

NOTE

The network does not make the list of corners directly. A list has a different length for each shape, and it has no fixed start point. These two facts make a list difficult to learn. Two images are easier. Section 08 shows how shapefit changes the two images into a list.

07Targets and losses

For each training pair, we draw the correct clean mask and the correct heatmap. A loss function measures the difference from the network output.

The clean mask target

shapefit draws the true polygon at 4 times the resolution, then reduces it. A pixel is inside if 50 % or more of its area is inside the polygon.

CAUTION

Do not draw the target with cv2.fillPoly at normal resolution. That function makes shapes approximately 0.45 pixels too large on each side. On a 128-pixel canvas, this decreases the accuracy by approximately 2 % IoU.

The heatmap target

Each corner gets a small round spot. The value is 1 at the corner pixel. The value decreases with distance, as a Gaussian with a width (σ) of 1.5 pixels.

The loss functions

A loss is one number that measures how wrong the network output is. Training changes the weights to make the loss smaller. shapefit adds three losses:

LossOutputWhat it does
Binary cross-entropyclean maskMeasures the error at each pixel. It gives a large penalty for a confident wrong answer.
Dice lossclean maskMeasures the overlap of the full shape. It keeps small shapes as important as large shapes.
Focal lossheatmapMeasures the error at each pixel, but gives less weight to easy pixels. Almost all pixels are "not a corner", so this is necessary.

Corner pixels are rare: approximately 6 in 16,384. A simple loss would teach the network to say "no corner" everywhere. The focal loss stops this. It also gives a smaller penalty to pixels that are near a corner, because those pixels are almost correct.

08From pixels to a polygon

The vectoriser changes the two output images into a list of corners. It uses geometry, not learning.

The vectoriser has five steps. Figure 9 shows the steps on one real example.

Step 1. Find the outline

Make the clean-mask output 4 times larger. Then find its outer edge as a closed chain of points. This chain is the contour. The larger image gives positions more accurate than one pixel.

Step 2. Find the peaks

A peak is a pixel that is higher than all 8 of its neighbours. Keep each peak with a value above 0.25. Calculate the centre of mass of the 3 × 3 pixels around it, for a more accurate position.

Step 3. Put the corners in order

Move each peak to the nearest point on the contour. Remove peaks that are more than 4 pixels from the contour. Each corner now has a position along the contour, as a distance from the start. This distance is the arclength. Sort the corners by arclength. The result is the correct order around the shape.

Step 4. Fit one line to each edge

The heatmap peaks are a little blurred. Thus shapefit does not use them directly as corners. For each edge, it takes the contour points between two corners. It ignores 15 % of the points at each end, because points near a corner are not on a straight part. Then it fits a straight line through the remaining points with total least squares.

Step 5. Intersect the lines

Each corner is the point where the lines of its two edges cross. This gives a sharp corner with an accuracy better than one pixel. If the two lines are almost parallel (less than 5°), or if the crossing point is more than 4 pixels from the peak, shapefit uses the peak position.

Optional: regularisation

Regularisation makes edge directions follow a rule. Use it for shapes made by people.

  • regularize="orthogonal" finds the main direction of the shape. Then it turns each edge to that direction or to 90° from it, if the difference is 25° or less. Use this for buildings and rooms.
  • regularize="parallel" finds edges that are almost parallel (12° or less). Then it makes them exactly parallel.
NOTE

The vectoriser has an upper limit of accuracy. To measure it, we gave it perfect network outputs (the targets). It then gets IoU 0.994 and the correct number of corners on 100 % of shapes. Thus nearly all remaining error comes from the network. The test is in tests/test_vectorize.py.

09Measuring quality

Five numbers compare the output polygon with the true polygon.

MetricBetterMeaning
IoUhigherArea of overlap divided by the area of the union. 1.0 is a perfect match.
Vertex count exacthigherPercent of shapes with exactly the correct number of corners.
Vertex F1 @ 3 %higherMatch output corners to true corners with Hungarian matching. A match is correct if the distance is 3 % of the shape size or less. F1 combines missed corners and extra corners in one number.
Vertex errorlowerMedian distance between matched corners, as a percent of the shape size.
HausdorfflowerThe largest distance from any point on one outline to the other outline. See Hausdorff distance.

IoU alone is not sufficient. A polygon with 25 small corners can have a good IoU, but it is not the correct polygon. Thus we also measure the corners.

10Demo results

The demo model learned 48 shapes perfectly. It does not work on new shapes yet. This is intentional.

We did not do the full training run yet. To show that all parts work together, we trained the network on only 48 shapes, for 2,500 steps. The network learns these shapes by memory. 11The full training run

One command starts the full run. It takes approximately 3.2 hours on an Apple M3 Max.

We measured the time with the default settings: 30,000 steps, a batch of 64 shapes, and the Apple GPU (MPS).

386 msper training step
0.4 msof that, waiting for data
1.6 sper check on 500 test shapes
≈ 3.2 hfor 30,000 steps (1.9 M shapes)

The GPU sets the speed. Eight CPU workers make the data much faster than the GPU uses it. Thus more workers will not make training faster.

Procedure

  1. Install the package: 
    pip install -e ".[dev]"
  2. Start the training: 
    python -m shapefit.train --steps 30000 --batch 64 --out runs/base
  3. Monitor runs/base/log.jsonl. Every 2,000 steps, the script writes the five metrics for 500 test shapes.
  4. Stop when IoU and "count exact" do not increase for several checks.
  5. Copy the best checkpoint into the package: 
    cp runs/base/best.pt shapefit/weights/shapefit.pt
  6. Use it. fit_polygon(mask) now loads this file automatically.
CAUTION

The training script cannot continue a stopped run yet. It saves last.pt every 2,000 steps, but a crash makes you start again. During our timing test, a GPU reset from a different program occurred one time.

To make training faster

We did not test these options yet:

  • Use fewer steps. 10,000 steps take approximately 1.1 hours.
  • Use bf16 mixed precision on MPS. This can be 1.5 to 2 times faster.
  • Use a smaller network: --base 24 or --base 16.
  • Use a CUDA GPU. This is probably faster than one hour.

Option: a fixed dataset on disk

You can also write a fixed dataset first. Each shape uses approximately 400 bytes, so 200,000 shapes use approximately 80 MB.

python -m shapefit.make_dataset --n 200000 --out data/train
python -m shapefit.train --data data/train --steps 30000 --out runs/shards

12Limits

shapefit does some tasks well and some tasks not at all. Know the limits before you use it.

  • One outer edge only. shapefit does not make polygons with holes. It ignores holes in the input, as noise.
  • One shape for each call. For many shapes, split the mask into connected components. Then call fit for each one.
  • Curves are not polygons. A circle or an ellipse gets many corners or unclear corners.
  • Resolution. The network sees 128 × 128 pixels. The line fit gives accuracy better than one pixel, but very small details on a large shape can disappear.
  • No general model yet. See section 11.

—Glossary

Each term has one meaning in this document.

Arclength
The distance along a curve from its start point.
Batch
A group of training pairs that the network uses together in one step. Here, 64 pairs.
Binary cross-entropy (BCE)
A loss for yes/no outputs. It gives a large penalty when the network is confident and wrong.
Bounding box
The smallest rectangle, with sides parallel to the image edges, that contains the shape.
Canvas
The fixed 128 × 128 image that the network always uses. See section 05.
Channel
One layer of values per pixel. A colour photo has 3 channels (red, green, blue). Inside a network, there can be hundreds.
Checkpoint
A file that contains the network weights at one point in training.
Connected component
A group of mask pixels that touch each other. Two shapes that do not touch are two components.
Contour
The outer edge of a mask, as a closed chain of points in order.
Convex hull
The smallest shape without dents that contains all the points. It looks like a rubber band around the points.
CoordConv
A method that adds the x and y position of each pixel as extra input channels.
Corner (vertex)
A point where two edges of a polygon meet.
Decoder
The half of a U-Net that makes the image larger again.
Dice loss
A loss that measures how much two areas overlap. It does not depend on the size of the shape.
Douglas–Peucker
A classic method that simplifies a line. It removes points that are nearer than ε to the line between their neighbours.
Edge
A straight side of a polygon, between two corners.
Encoder
The half of a U-Net that makes the image smaller and finds larger patterns.
ε (epsilon)
The distance limit in Douglas–Peucker. Here it is a percent of the outline length.
F1
A score from 0 to 1 that combines precision (how many found corners are correct) and recall (how many true corners are found).
Focal loss
A loss that gives less weight to easy pixels. It is used when the important pixels are rare.
Gaussian
A smooth, bell-shaped curve. It is highest at the centre and decreases with distance.
Hausdorff distance
The largest distance from a point on one outline to the nearest point on the other outline. It shows the worst error.
Heatmap
An image where each pixel value shows how probable something is at that position. Here: a corner.
Hungarian matching
A method that pairs two sets of points one to one, with the smallest total distance.
Input
The data that goes into the network. Here: the damaged mask on the canvas.
IoU (intersection over union)
The overlap area of two shapes divided by the area that either shape covers. 1.0 means identical.
Logit
A raw network output, from minus infinity to plus infinity. The sigmoid function changes it to a probability from 0 to 1.
Loss
One number that shows how wrong the network output is. Training makes it smaller.
Mask
An image where each pixel is 1 (inside the shape) or 0 (outside the shape).
Morphology
Image operations that grow or shrink a mask by a small shape. "Open" removes small parts. "Close" fills small gaps.
MPS
Metal Performance Shaders. The system that lets PyTorch use the GPU on an Apple computer.
Neural network
A large function with many adjustable weights. Training adjusts the weights so that the function gives the wanted outputs.
Normal
The direction at 90° to an edge, out of the shape or into the shape.
Outline
The boundary of a shape. For a mask, the outline is the contour.
Overfitting
A network learns its training examples by memory and does not work on new examples.
Peak
A heatmap pixel with a higher value than all 8 neighbours.
Pixel
One small square of an image. Here, pixel centres are at whole-number positions (x = column, y = row).
Polygon
A closed shape made of straight edges.
Probability
How frequently something occurs, from 0 % (never) to 100 % (always).
Regularisation
Here: a rule that changes edge directions, for example to make all corners 90°.
Severity
A random value for each training pair that controls how strong the damage is. Its mean is 1.
Skip connection
A path in a U-Net that copies detail from an encoder step to the decoder step of the same size.
Step
One update of the weights, from one batch.
Synthetic data
Data that a program makes, not data from the real world.
Target
The correct answer for one training input.
Total least squares
A line fit that makes the perpendicular distances from the points to the line as small as possible.
Training
The process that adjusts the network weights to make the loss smaller.
TTA (test-time augmentation)
Run the network on rotated and flipped copies of one input, then calculate the mean of the results.
Turn angle
The change of direction at a corner. 0° means a straight line.
U-Net
A network that makes an image smaller, then larger again, with skip connections. Its diagram has a U shape.
Vectoriser
The part of shapefit that changes the network output images into a list of corners.
Weights
The adjustable numbers inside a neural network.
